# Rubric

100 points across six areas. The weights do not change with seniority. What
changes is the bar for full marks in each area.

The arithmetic belongs in the report: points awarded per area, then the total.
A total is never nudged toward a band that reads better.

| Area | Points |
|---|---|
| Case study clarity | 20 |
| Valid data and outcomes | 20 |
| Visual craft | 20 |
| First scan and organization | 15 |
| Accessibility | 15 |
| Role and level fit | 10 |

## Level bands

Three bars. Place the designer by the target role they gave you. On "general,"
use the band the portfolio itself reads as and say so.

- **Junior and mid**: first role through about five years.
- **Senior**: senior individual contributor.
- **Lead and above**: lead, staff, principal, manager, director.

The scope expectations come from VMware Design (see `evidence.md`): a junior
shows complete process on a single feature, a lead shows a good size product or
a portfolio of products, and a leader shows how they influenced others.

## 1. Case study clarity (20)

What it measures: can a reviewer follow the thinking without asking questions?

Check against NN/g's seven parts, but do not score them as a checklist. A short
case study that covers the problem, the decisions, the role, and the result beats
a long one that walks through every phase. Meta's point stands: open with the
most compelling material, because readers drop off.

- **18 to 20**: The problem is specific. Decisions are visible with the reasoning
  behind them. At least one trade-off or rejected option is named. The reader
  never has to guess what happened or why.
- **14 to 17**: Problem and solution are clear. Reasoning is thin in places, or
  one case study carries the whole portfolio while the others are thumbnails.
- **9 to 13**: Reads as a sequence of steps or artifacts. The reader can see what
  was made but not why. Or it is long enough that the point gets buried.
- **4 to 8**: Screens with captions. No problem statement, or a generic one.
- **0 to 3**: No case study a reviewer could follow.

Bar by level:
- Junior and mid: sound reasoning on one feature or flow is enough.
- Senior: at least one case study shows a decision that could have gone another
  way, and why it went this way.
- Lead and above: shows judgment across more than one project, team, or product,
  including what was chosen not to do.

Do not deduct for a short case study. Do not ask for more process. Ask for the
reasoning that is missing.

## 2. Valid data and outcomes (20)

What it measures: are the numbers real and attributable, and is there a result at
all?

Run the metrics audit on every number claimed. For each one, check four things:

1. **Baseline.** What was it before? "Increased conversion 23%" with no starting
   point cannot be read.
2. **Timeframe.** Over what period, and measured when?
3. **Source.** Where did the number come from? Analytics, a test platform, a
   support log, a survey?
4. **Share.** What was this designer's part in it, and what else changed at the
   same time?

Put the audit in the report as a table. One row per number. Mark each of the four
as present or missing. This is the most useful part of the review for anyone
making outcome claims, and the most common thing that falls apart in an
interview.

- **18 to 20**: Outcomes are stated and the numbers survive the audit. Anything
  still unreleased comes with the measurement plan attached.
- **14 to 17**: Outcomes are there, with one or two gaps in the audit, such as a
  number with no timeframe.
- **9 to 13**: Outcomes are vague ("users loved it," "improved the experience"),
  or numbers appear with none of the four.
- **4 to 8**: No outcome of any kind, and no measurement plan.
- **0 to 3**: Numbers that look invented or cannot be reconciled with the work
  described.

Bar by level:
- Junior and mid: a qualitative result or a usability finding that changed the
  design counts. A measurement plan counts.
- Senior: at least one case study ties the work to a user or business metric.
- Lead and above: the connection to business outcomes is explicit, and the
  numbers hold up.

Fairness: missing metrics are common and often outside a designer's control. A
missing number is a gap to fix, not dishonesty. A number that fails the audit is
a risk to the designer in an interview, and that is how to frame it. Never accuse
anyone of fabricating anything.

## 3. Visual craft (20)

What it measures: does the portfolio itself demonstrate the craft it claims?

This is where the portfolio is judged as a designed object. Meta's point applies:
a designer should be able to justify color, typography, and each design decision.

Check:

- **Spacing.** Are margins and padding consistent across pages and within
  repeated elements? Do cards in a grid use the same internal padding? Does the
  page have a consistent container width? Name the specific place it breaks.
- **Type hierarchy.** Is there a clear and consistent order among heading levels
  and body text? Can you tell an h2 from an h3 by looking? Is there a jump where
  two levels look identical, or six sizes doing the work of three?
- **Body text.** Is it large enough to read comfortably, and is the line length
  reasonable? Very long lines are hard to track.
- **Alignment and rhythm.** Do elements line up to a consistent grid? Is vertical
  spacing between sections consistent?
- **Image treatment.** Are screenshots and thumbnails consistent in size,
  background, crop, and shadow? Figma's recruiter calls this out directly: a
  consistent grid is easier to scan.
- **Typos.** Proofread the copy you read. Spelling and grammar errors get noticed
  and read as weak attention to detail.

- **18 to 20**: Consistent spacing, clear type hierarchy, consistent image
  treatment, no errors found. Deliberate choices, even if plain.
- **14 to 17**: Mostly consistent with specific lapses you can name.
- **9 to 13**: Visible inconsistency in spacing or hierarchy, or several typos.
- **4 to 8**: Spacing and hierarchy are not controlled. Hard to read.
- **0 to 3**: Illegible or broken layout.

Bar by level: the same at every level. Craft is craft. A junior portfolio with
clean spacing and a clear hierarchy scores full marks here.

Fairness: plain is not a deduction. Bold is not a bonus. A restrained layout
executed cleanly beats an ambitious one executed loosely. Do not push anyone
toward a trend. If the portfolio has a distinctive choice of its own, name it
under "Worth keeping" so the fixes do not sand it off.

## 4. First scan and organization (15)

What it measures: in the first thirty seconds, can a reviewer tell who this is,
what they do, and where to click?

Reviewers do not read on the first pass. They scan, and the window is short. See
the UXfolio figures in `evidence.md`.

Check:

- Who the person is and what they do, visible without scrolling or hunting.
- Work reachable in one click. Nothing buried behind an About page, a hidden
  menu, or a forced choice.
- Three to five projects. Both NN/g and Meta land on that range.
- Cards that carry context: what the project is, who it was for, and the
  designer's role. A grid of pretty thumbnails with one-word titles fails this.
- Navigation that holds up on a second page, and a way back.
- No auto-scrolling, horizontal scrolling, or carousels that get in the way.
- Load time. A slow first paint costs real reviewers.

- **14 to 15**: Clear in seconds. Work one click away. Cards carry context.
- **11 to 13**: Clear, with one friction point, such as thin card copy.
- **7 to 10**: Takes effort to work out who they are or where the work is. Or
  there are too many projects, or only one.
- **3 to 6**: Confusing structure. The work is hard to find.
- **0 to 2**: Cannot navigate it.

## 5. Accessibility (15)

What it measures: can people with low vision, color blindness, or a keyboard use
this site?

Score only what you can check. Say what you checked and how.

Weight the checks by what they block. A focus indicator nobody can see stops a
keyboard user from using the site at all. A missing alt attribute on a decorative
image stops no one. The points follow that.

- **Text contrast (up to 5)**: Sample the body text, headings, link text, button
  labels, and any text over an image or gradient. Report the ratio and the pass
  or fail against 4.5:1 normal and 3:1 large. Name the exact colors and the
  element.
- **Focus and non-text contrast (up to 5)**: The focus indicator, buttons, form
  field borders, and icons that carry meaning need 3:1 against adjacent colors.
  A focus ring below 3:1 scores 0 here on its own, whatever else passes, because
  it makes keyboard navigation invisible.
- **Keyboard operation (up to 2)**: Can the main navigation and every case study
  be reached and opened by keyboard? Does focus move into a modal when one opens,
  and back out when it closes?
- **Images and alt text (up to 2)**: Do meaningful images have alt text? Are
  decorative ones marked as decorative? Is any important text baked into an image
  with no text equivalent?
- **Headings and structure (up to 1)**: Is there one h1? Do heading levels
  descend in order without skipping? Are headings real headings rather than
  styled divs?

Report as "passes WCAG 2.2 AA" or "fails WCAG 2.2 AA" on the specific checks you
ran. Never say "ADA compliant." Never claim the whole site is accessible on the
basis of these checks. Say what you tested.

**You may not score a contrast check you did not measure.** Sample the computed
foreground and background colors and compute the ratio. If you could not read the
colors, the contrast checks are unscorable, and their points leave the total
rather than being awarded. See "Unscorable checks" below. Never estimate a ratio,
and never give points for a check you did not run.

## 6. Role and level fit (10)

What it measures: is ownership clear, and does the scope shown match the role
being targeted?

- **Ownership (up to 6)**: Is there a plain statement of what this designer did
  and what the team did? A line naming the role, the collaborators, and the
  timeline earns full marks. "We" throughout with no role stated does not.
  Claiming every discipline on a large project reads as implausible and should be
  flagged gently as a risk in interview, not as a lie.
- **Scope match (up to 4)**: Does the work shown reach the scope the target role
  implies? Use the VMware ladder in `evidence.md`. Also check relevance: does the
  work relate to the kind of product and role being targeted?

On a stated target, say the gap plainly: what reads as present, what a reviewer
for that level would also expect, and the nearest thing in the portfolio that
could be developed to close it.

On "general," score ownership normally and score scope against what the portfolio
presents itself as.

## Unscorable checks

A check you could not run is not a check the portfolio passed.

When something cannot be tested, its points come **out of the total available**.
They are never awarded and never counted as a failure. Say in the header what was
not assessed and what the score is out of.

- Could not measure contrast but ran the other accessibility checks: drop the 10
  contrast points. The report scores out of 90.
- Could not run any accessibility check: drop all 15. The report scores out of
  85, and the header says accessibility was not assessed.
- No browser at all: this is the source-only review. Drop visual craft and
  accessibility, 35 points, and score out of 65.

Then convert to a percentage of the points available and read the band off that,
as described under Verdict bands. Show both numbers.

The failure this prevents: a review that measures nothing and awards most of the
points scores a portfolio higher than a review that measures everything and finds
real problems. If you cannot test it, take it off the board. Do not price it in.

This carries into the scoring table. The area's Points cell shows what was
actually scorable, not its full weight, and its Given cell shows a number. An
accessibility row that kept 3 of its 15 points reads 3 and 2, never 15 and
"2 of 3 assessed". Both columns are bare numbers that add up to the totals
beneath them, so a reader can check the arithmetic in their head.

## Deal-breakers

These replace red-flag point deductions. They are structural, not stylistic.

- **Broken or unreachable link**: do not score. Report what failed, with the
  exact URL and error. Stop.
- **Password required and not supplied**: do not score. Ask for the password and
  stop. If it arrives, review normally and note in the report that the site
  requires a password, since that stops real reviewers.
- **Download required to see the work**: score normally but cap the total at 70,
  and name it as the first fix. A reviewer who has to download a file often
  stops.
- **Renders blank or broken for a returning visitor**: cap the total at 70, and
  make it the first fix. A site that works on a hard refresh but serves a stale
  or broken page to someone who visited before is broken for exactly the people
  most likely to come back. Check whether the HTML carries a `Cache-Control`
  header and whether the bundle it references still exists on the server.
- **No readable case study anywhere**: cap the total at 30. Without a case study
  there is nothing to judge on clarity, outcomes, or ownership, which is 50 of
  the 100 points.

Say in the report when a cap was applied and why.

## Verdict bands

Bands are percentages of the points available, never raw point totals. Write
them with the percent sign everywhere they appear, so nobody reads 85 as a
point score.

- **85% to 100%**: Send it.
- **70% to 84%**: Send after fixes.
- **50% to 69%**: Fix before sending.
- **Under 50%**: Rework.

In a source-only review the total available is 65, not 100. Convert the score to
a percentage of the points available and apply the bands to that. Show both
numbers, for example "45/65, which is 69%." Say that visual craft and
accessibility were not scored, so the percentage reflects content only.

State the band and one sentence on what it means. Do not soften a low band with
praise that is not in the findings, and do not inflate a score to avoid
delivering one.

## Explaining the score

A number on its own tells someone almost nothing. Three things go under the score
line, in this order.

### Why the total is what it is

Only when points were removed because a check could not be run. Name each one and
what it was worth:

> **Why 85, not 100:** Fifteen accessibility points were left out. I could not
> read the computed colors, so text contrast (5), focus and non-text contrast
> (5), keyboard operation (2), alt text (2) and heading structure (1) all went
> untested. Those points were left out rather than guessed, so this score covers
> only what was actually measured. Open checks below says how to get them scored.

Skip this entirely when the review was complete and the total available was 100.
Never apologize for it, and never imply the missing points would have been
earned or lost. They were not measured, which is a different thing from either.

### What the band means

A four-row table with their row marked:

| Band | Range | |
|---|---|---|
| Send it | 85% to 100% | |
| Send after fixes | 70% to 84% | You are here |
| Fix before sending | 50% to 69% | |
| Rework | Under 50% | |

Then one sentence on where inside the band they sit and what would move them up.
"79% is the top of Send after fixes, and the leadership case study is the single
thing that moves it into Send it." A score two percentage points below a boundary should
say so, because that is actionable in a way the number alone is not.

### Context, only where it is earned

A figure from `evidence.md` belongs here when it bears directly on something this
review found. If the first scan was slow, the UXfolio finding that 65% of
reviewers spend under four minutes explains what that costs. Cite the source by
name, as always.

**Never compare this score to a published statistic.** No published average
portfolio score exists, so any comparison would be invented. A figure about how
long reviewers spend, or how many portfolios they see, is context about the
reader. It is not a benchmark for this total, and presenting it next to the score
invites exactly that misreading.

Leave this out when no figure genuinely applies. An irrelevant statistic makes
the report longer and the reasoning weaker.

## Weights are this skill's own

Say so in the report, in one line. The areas are informed by NN/g, Meta, Figma's
design recruiter, VMware Design, and WCAG. The point values are this skill's
judgment and are not endorsed by any of them.
