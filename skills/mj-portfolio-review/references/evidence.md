# Evidence

Everything below was read from the source named beside it. Name that source in
the report when you use it. Checks that rest on nothing published are labeled
judgment calls instead. Figures stay exactly as published: no rounding, no
merging two statistics into one, no filling a gap with a plausible number.

## Nielsen Norman Group

Source: https://nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/
Basis: a survey of 204 UX professionals in charge of hiring.

- Process over polish. Hiring managers want to see the work behind the final
  design, not just the finished product. One respondent: "Don't just show me the
  finished product."
- Seven parts recommended for each case study:
  1. The problem or hypothesis
  2. Your specific role and collaboration
  3. How you reached the solution
  4. Whether it solved the problem
  5. Challenges, including concepts not pursued
  6. The effect on users and the business
  7. What you learned
- Pick 3 to 5 detailed case studies. Quality over quantity, and show a range of
  skills.
- Keep the portfolio scannable and avoid flashy templates. Use relevant images
  such as sketches, whiteboards, research documentation, and final screens, and
  break up text with visuals.
- State your specific role and how you worked with others.

Note: NN/g's article does not break its findings down by seniority. Do not
attribute a seniority claim to NN/g.

## Meta Design

Source: https://www.meta.com/design-at-meta/blog/5-ways-to-improve-your-design-portfolio-today/

- Reviewers look for a clear human problem backed by research or data, the
  business reason it matters, the real outcome and whether it met its metrics,
  and what the designer would do differently now.
- Be ready to justify color, typography, and each design decision.
- Include three to five projects, each highlighting a different strength, rather
  than 20 or more.
- Process gets no more than a sentence online. Expand on it only if it was
  unusual or directly drove the outcome.
- An audit by the author found the average project exceeded 1,300 words. The
  advice is to cut toward 500, 200, or even 100.
- Avoid the standard overview, process, solution, outcome template with a header
  and image for each. Open with the most compelling points so they land before a
  reader drops off.
- A "learn more" link is useful for depth, but hiring managers often will not
  click it, so the core story must stand on its own.

Note: Meta advises spending little space on process. NN/g advises showing the
work behind the final design. These are not actually in conflict: judge the
quality of the reasoning shown, not the quantity of process artifacts. Never
tell a designer to add more process steps. Tell them what reasoning is missing.

## Figma design recruiter

Source: https://dribbble.com/resources/career/design-recruiter-portfolio-tips
Korin Harris, design recruiter at Figma.

- Make the work reachable in one click. Reviewers will not search for it, so
  avoid burying projects behind "about" content, hidden menus, or a forced
  choice.
- Keep project previews visually consistent so the grid is easy to scan. Use
  matching sizes for logos, or matching backgrounds and shadows for screenshots.
- Label each project with the same details, such as company, role, and medium.
- Each project should tell a complete story: the problem, how the solution was
  reached, the iterations, and the designer's specific role.
- Keep copy minimal and plain. Heavy text blocks suggest oversharing. Let the
  visuals carry the information.
- Avoid auto-scrolling, horizontal scrolling, and carousels when they hurt
  usability.
- Proofread. Spelling errors are noticed quickly and read as weak attention to
  detail.

## VMware Design: how the bar moves with level

Source: https://medium.com/vmwaredesign/product-design-portfolios-63618e1692b6

- Junior: solid work on a single feature is fine as long as it shows a complete
  design process.
- Design lead: work on a good size product, or even a portfolio of products.
- Design leader and manager: documentation of how they have influenced others.

This is the backbone of the level read. Judge scope, not polish.

## WCAG 2.2 contrast, Level AA

Sources: https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/ and
https://www.getstark.co/wcag-explained/perceivable/distinguishable/contrast-minimum/

- 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum): normal text needs at least 4.5:1 against its
  background.
- Large text needs at least 3:1. WCAG defines large text as 18pt and larger, or
  14pt and larger if bold. In CSS terms that is roughly 24px, or 18.66px bold.
- 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast: user interface components and graphical objects need
  at least 3:1 against adjacent colors.
- Exempt: text in logos or brand names, and purely decorative text.
- 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced) is Level AAA and asks for 7:1 normal and 4.5:1
  large. Do not score against AAA. Mention it only as an option.

Say "passes WCAG 2.2 AA contrast" or "fails it." Do not say "ADA compliant."
The ADA does not set contrast ratios, and that phrasing makes a legal claim this
skill is not in a position to make.

## UXfolio, State of UX Portfolios 2026

Source: https://uxfol.io/state-of-ux-portfolios-2026/report
Basis: UXfolio surveyed more than 100 hiring managers and design leaders who
reviewed over 5,000 portfolios, then interviewed several in depth
(per Uxcel's writeup: https://uxcel.com/blog/ux-portfolio-review-red-flags-green-flags).

Only these figures were read directly from the report and may be cited:

- 78% rely on the portfolio and case studies to judge whether a candidate is a
  fit.
- 65% of reviewers spend under four minutes on a portfolio the first time they
  open it. The report describes a first-pass scan of 1 to 4 minutes, and up to
  30 minutes of close attention for shortlisted case studies.
- 53% of reviewers rate an About section as important.
- One in ten reviewers reviewed more than 200 portfolios last year.

Further statistics from this report circulate secondhand, among them a ranked
red-flag list with percentages attached. None of those were confirmed against the
original, so they stay out. The test is simple: having actually read a figure is
what earns the right to quote it. Everything else is a judgment call.
